Swelling after oral surgery is a predictable inflammatory response, not a sign of infection or complication. Understanding the normal timeline helps patients distinguish expected post-operative changes from signs that warrant attention.

## The Standard Swelling Timeline

Regardless of procedure type, swelling peaks approximately 48 to 72 hours after surgery — not immediately after the procedure. Patients often notice that swelling is worse on day two or three than it was on day one. This is normal and expected.

After the peak, swelling begins to subside gradually. For most procedures, visible swelling is substantially reduced by days five to seven. Residual firmness or mild puffiness in the cheek or jaw area may persist for one to two weeks, particularly after surgical extractions or implant placement.

## Swelling by Procedure Type

**Simple tooth extraction.** Minimal to moderate swelling, typically resolving within five to seven days. Swelling may be barely noticeable for uncomplicated single extractions under local anesthesia.

**Wisdom tooth removal (surgical).** Often the most swollen procedure patients experience. The combination of impaction depth, bone removal, and flap access commonly produces significant facial swelling. Patients routinely experience noticeable swelling for seven to ten days. Jaw stiffness (trismus) often accompanies lower wisdom tooth removal and can last one to two weeks.

**Bone grafting.** Similar to surgical extraction. Five to seven days for visible resolution, with residual firmness for a few days beyond that.

**Single dental implant placement.** Moderate swelling, typically resolving within five to seven days.

**Full-arch surgery (All-on-4, multiple extractions, complex bone work).** Swelling can be more extensive and longer-lasting, with visible resolution taking ten to fourteen days or more depending on the scope of surgery.

## Swelling Management

- **Cold packs during the first 24 hours**: apply for 20 minutes on, 20 minutes off. After 24 hours, cold is less effective and some surgeons transition to heat.
- **Sleeping elevated**: keeping the head above the heart reduces fluid accumulation. Use extra pillows or a recliner for the first few nights.
- **Prescribed medications**: anti-inflammatory medication and corticosteroids (when prescribed) significantly reduce post-operative swelling.
- **Avoiding activity**: physical exertion increases blood flow and inflammation. Light activity for the first few days reduces swelling duration.

## When Swelling Is Abnormal

Swelling that worsens after day three to four — rather than improving — is abnormal. Additional warning signs that warrant immediate contact with the surgeon:

- Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) accompanying increasing swelling
- Swelling spreading toward the neck, throat, or floor of the mouth
- Difficulty swallowing or difficulty breathing (emergency: seek immediate care)
- Pus or discharge from the surgical site
- Severe pain that is not controlled by prescribed medication

Spread of infection from an oral surgery site toward the airway can develop quickly. If swelling is spreading to the neck or you have difficulty swallowing or opening your mouth significantly beyond what your surgeon described, seek emergency evaluation.
