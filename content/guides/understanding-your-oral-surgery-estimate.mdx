## Introduction

An oral surgery estimate is not a standardized document. Different practices format estimates differently, include different line items, and use different billing codes. Understanding what a complete, transparent estimate should contain — and what to ask when something is missing — helps patients compare options more accurately and avoid surprises.

## What a Complete Estimate Should Include

A useful written estimate identifies each component of the planned treatment separately, with a fee associated with each.

**The surgical procedure itself**: The primary procedure — extraction, implant placement, bone grafting, or other surgery — should appear as a line item with its associated fee and the relevant billing code.

**Imaging and diagnostic fees**: X-rays, panoramic imaging, or cone beam CT scans may be billed separately or included. The estimate should clarify which imaging is needed and whether it is included.

**Anesthesia and sedation**: If sedation is planned, the type of sedation and its cost should be itemized separately. Sedation fees are often calculated by time increments, and the estimate should reflect the anticipated duration.

**Bone grafting or socket preservation**: If grafting is part of the treatment plan, it should appear as a separate line item with the graft material type noted.

**Implant components**: For implant treatment, the estimate should distinguish between the implant post placed by the surgeon, the abutment connector piece, and the final restoration crown. These may be placed by different providers and billed separately.

**Follow-up visits**: Post-operative visits and monitoring appointments may or may not be included. Clarify what is and is not covered within the quoted fee.

## What Low Estimates Often Omit

Advertised or quoted prices that appear significantly lower than others often reflect partial treatment — the surgical fee alone, without imaging, sedation, grafting, or the final restoration. Comparing a partial estimate to a comprehensive one is not an equivalent comparison.

Common omissions in low-cost estimates include:

- Sedation fees listed separately or not listed at all
- Pre-surgical imaging billed at the time of the appointment
- Socket preservation or bone grafting identified only after extraction
- The crown and abutment excluded from an implant quote
- Follow-up appointments billed per visit

## What to Request Before Treatment

When reviewing an estimate, ask the provider or coordinator to confirm what is included and what is not, whether there are any procedures that may be recommended after the initial examination that would add to this total, whether imaging is included or billed separately, whether sedation is included and if so what type and duration, and whether bone grafting is reflected in the estimate if it may be needed.

Request the estimate in writing before agreeing to treatment. A provider who is unwilling to put the fee structure in writing before treatment is a significant concern.

## How to Compare Estimates from Multiple Providers

Before comparing numbers, verify that each estimate covers the same scope of treatment. The most useful comparison is not the total dollar amount — it is what that amount includes.

Consider asking each provider to provide an itemized estimate using the same set of assumptions: same procedure scope, same sedation type, same imaging, and same follow-up expectations. This makes comparison meaningful.

## Questions to Ask Before Agreeing to Treatment

- Is this estimate itemized, or is it a bundled total?
- What happens to the estimate if the scope of treatment changes during surgery?
- What is your policy on estimates that change between the time of quoting and the day of surgery?
- Will this estimate change if I use insurance, and how will the final patient balance be calculated?
