## Introduction

A missing tooth is often treated as a cosmetic issue, but the effects extend beyond appearance. Over time, a gap left by a missing tooth can lead to structural changes in the jaw and mouth that complicate or limit future treatment options.

## How Bone Loss Develops After Tooth Loss

When a tooth root is no longer present in the jawbone, the bone around the empty socket gradually loses density. This process, called resorption, begins shortly after extraction and continues over months and years. The bone is maintained in part by the mechanical stimulation from a functioning tooth root. Without that stimulation, the surrounding bone has no functional reason to maintain its density.

This matters because dental implants require adequate bone volume to place and stabilize the implant post. A patient who delays implant treatment may eventually require bone grafting — which would not have been necessary if treatment had proceeded sooner or if socket preservation had been performed at the time of extraction.

## Teeth Can Shift Into the Gap

Neighboring teeth may tilt or drift toward the empty space over time. The opposing tooth — the one on the other jaw that used to contact the missing tooth — may over-erupt, meaning it begins to push further into the mouth since there is no longer a surface to bite against.

These shifts affect bite alignment, can make it harder to clean certain teeth, and may complicate future restorative treatment by narrowing the available space for replacement.

## Impact on Implant Eligibility

Whether a patient remains a candidate for a dental implant depends partly on how much bone remains at the site. Significant bone loss may require augmentation procedures such as ridge augmentation or a sinus lift before an implant can be placed. These procedures add time, cost, and surgical complexity to the treatment path.

Socket preservation, performed at the time of extraction, is one way patients can maintain bone volume while they decide on a replacement strategy. This is a question worth raising with a provider at the time any tooth is extracted.

## Questions to Ask Your Provider

- How much bone volume is currently present at this site?
- Has any resorption begun, and how far has it progressed?
- Would socket preservation at extraction have changed my current options?
- What are my current replacement options, and do any require bone grafting first?
- What is the expected cost difference between treating now versus waiting longer?
