## Introduction

Bone grafting in oral surgery involves placing material into an area where bone has been lost or is insufficient. In New York, patients most commonly encounter bone grafting as part of preparation for dental implant placement or at the time of tooth extraction to preserve the site.

This guide explains when bone grafting may be relevant, what the procedure involves, and what patients should understand before consultation.

## What Bone Grafting Does

When a tooth is lost or extracted, the surrounding bone can resorb — meaning the body begins to break down bone that is no longer stimulated by a tooth root. Over time, this can reduce the volume and density of bone in that area.

Bone grafting introduces material that supports the body's own bone-regeneration process. The goal is to maintain or restore enough bone structure to support future treatment, most commonly implant placement.

## Types of Bone Grafting Procedures

Several distinct procedures fall under the term "bone grafting" in oral surgery:

**Socket preservation (ridge preservation):** Graft material placed into the extraction socket at the time of tooth removal to reduce bone loss during healing. This is a common procedure when future implant placement is being considered.

**Ridge augmentation:** A procedure to restore bone width or height in areas where significant resorption has already occurred. More complex than socket preservation and typically involves a healing period before implant placement.

**Sinus lift (sinus augmentation):** A procedure to add bone below the sinus floor in the upper jaw, used when the sinus has expanded into an area where implants are being planned. Requires specific provider training.

**Block grafts:** Transfer of bone from another site in the mouth or from an external source, used in cases requiring larger volume additions.

Each of these has different complexity, recovery timelines, and cost implications.

## Graft Material

Bone graft material comes from several sources:

- **Autograft:** Bone taken from the patient's own body. Considered a strong biological option but requires a second surgical site.
- **Allograft:** Processed bone from a human donor source. Commonly used in socket preservation and ridge procedures.
- **Xenograft:** Processed animal-derived bone, typically bovine. Widely used in clinical practice.
- **Synthetic (alloplast):** Manufactured materials that serve as scaffolding for bone regeneration.

The choice of material is a clinical decision influenced by the case, the provider's approach, and the patient's health.

## When Bone Grafting May Be Discussed

Bone grafting may come up when:

- a tooth is being extracted and implant placement is a future goal
- implant planning reveals insufficient bone volume
- a sinus lift is required for upper-jaw implant placement
- ridge atrophy has occurred after long-term tooth loss
- trauma has resulted in bone loss

Whether grafting is necessary — and which type — is determined through clinical evaluation, imaging (often including CBCT), and treatment planning discussion with a provider.

## Cost Factors in New York

Bone grafting cost in New York varies based on:

- the type of graft procedure
- the size and complexity of the site
- the material used (autograft from another surgical site adds cost)
- whether membrane use is included
- sedation or anesthesia approach
- imaging and follow-up structure
- borough and practice setting

Socket preservation at the time of extraction is generally less complex and less expensive than ridge augmentation or sinus lift procedures. Itemized written estimates allow patients to understand what is and is not included.

## Questions to Ask Before Treatment

Before agreeing to bone grafting, patients should consider asking:

- Why is bone grafting recommended in my case?
- What type of graft is being proposed, and what are the options?
- What material will be used, and where does it come from?
- What is the timeline between grafting and implant placement?
- What are the risks specific to this type of graft in my situation?
- What happens if I do not pursue grafting?
- Is imaging (CBCT) needed, and is it included in the cost?
- What does recovery involve, and what is the expected healing period?

## Recovery Considerations

Recovery after bone grafting depends on the complexity of the procedure:

- Socket preservation typically involves a routine post-extraction recovery period
- Sinus lifts and ridge augmentation procedures may require longer healing and specific precautions
- Patients are generally instructed to avoid disturbing the graft site
- Follow-up imaging is typically used to assess graft integration before implant placement proceeds
