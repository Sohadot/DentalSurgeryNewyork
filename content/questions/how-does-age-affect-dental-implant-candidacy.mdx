Age affects dental implant candidacy at both ends of the spectrum, though in different ways and for different reasons. There is no upper age limit for implants — patients in their 80s and 90s can be successful implant candidates if their bone and systemic health support it. At the younger end, jaw growth must be complete before implants are placed.

## Why Implants Cannot Be Placed Before Jaw Growth Is Complete

Dental implants integrate directly with the jawbone and do not move once placed. Natural teeth, by contrast, continue to erupt and shift as the jaw develops. If an implant is placed while jaw growth is still active, the surrounding teeth continue to develop while the implant remains fixed. This causes the implant to appear submerged or to fall out of alignment with adjacent teeth over time — a cosmetic and functional problem that may require implant removal and replacement.

Jaw growth is generally complete at approximately 17–18 years for females and 18–21 years for males, but individual variation is significant. Growth completion can be confirmed by comparing serial panoramic radiographs taken at least six months apart with no change in jaw structure.

For patients who lose a tooth before jaw growth is complete, a removable partial denture or a resin-bonded bridge can serve as a temporary replacement until implant placement is appropriate.

## Age and Bone Volume in Older Adults

There is no age at which implants are categorically contraindicated in older adults. Bone volume and density decline gradually with age, and significant bone resorption following long-term tooth loss or denture wear can reduce the available bone for implant support. CBCT imaging evaluates actual bone volume, which varies widely between individuals of the same age.

Age-related bone changes do not prevent implant placement in most cases. When bone volume is insufficient, grafting — performed in advance or simultaneously — can restore the necessary architecture.

## Systemic Health and Medications Matter More Than Age Itself

The relevant considerations for older adults are less about age and more about the systemic health conditions and medications that become more prevalent with age:

**Bisphosphonate medications.** Used for osteoporosis (alendronate/Fosamax, risedronate, zoledronic acid) and some cancer treatments. These drugs reduce bone remodeling, which is necessary for osseointegration and healing. Long-term use of oral bisphosphonates and any use of IV bisphosphonates significantly increases the risk of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) — a serious complication that can follow oral surgery. All bisphosphonate use must be disclosed before implant evaluation.

**Diabetes.** Affects healing, infection risk, and osseointegration. Controlled diabetes (HbA1c within acceptable range) does not categorically prevent implant placement, but uncontrolled diabetes substantially increases complication risk.

**Cardiovascular disease and anticoagulants.** Managed cardiovascular disease is not a contraindication. Anticoagulant medications require surgical management planning (see the blood thinners and oral surgery page).

**Ability to maintain oral hygiene.** Long-term implant health depends on daily home care and professional maintenance. Patients with conditions that limit manual dexterity or cognitive capacity to maintain hygiene face higher long-term peri-implantitis risk.

## Insurance and Medicare

Medicare does not cover dental implants. Age does not trigger any new implant-related insurance coverage. Patients transitioning to Medicare should confirm their dental coverage separately, as Medicare Part A and Part B do not cover routine dental care or implants.
