Nerve damage after oral surgery refers to injury to a sensory nerve in the jaw or mouth, resulting in numbness, tingling, or altered sensation in the areas that nerve supplies. It is one of the material risks disclosed to patients before lower wisdom tooth removal and lower implant placement — two procedures where nerve proximity is greatest.

## Which Nerves Are at Risk

**Inferior alveolar nerve (IAN).** Runs through the lower jaw inside the mandibular canal, supplying sensation to the lower teeth, lower lip, and chin on the affected side. This nerve is most commonly at risk during surgical removal of deeply impacted lower wisdom teeth, where roots may curve around or contact the canal. It is also at risk during lower posterior implant placement when the planned implant depth approaches the canal — the accepted safety margin is typically a minimum of 2mm between the implant apex and the superior wall of the canal.

**Lingual nerve.** Runs along the inner surface of the lower jaw near the floor of the mouth, supplying sensation to the tongue, floor of the mouth, and lingual gingiva. It is at risk during lower wisdom tooth removal, particularly with certain flap designs and bone removal approaches.

## Types of Nerve Injury

Nerve injuries exist on a spectrum:

- **Neuropraxia** — Temporary disruption from pressure, stretching, or local anesthetic proximity, without structural damage to the nerve itself. Sensation returns fully. The most common outcome after lower wisdom tooth removal; typically resolves within weeks to a few months.
- **Axonotmesis** — Damage to nerve fibers within an intact outer sheath. Recovery is possible but slower, over months. Outcome is variable depending on injury severity.
- **Neurotmesis** — Severance or significant structural disruption of the nerve. Rare in elective oral surgery. Recovery without microsurgical intervention is unlikely.

## Symptoms

- Numbness or reduced sensation in the lower lip, chin, or tongue
- Tingling or pins-and-needles sensation (paresthesia)
- Burning or painful sensation in the affected area (dysesthesia) — less common but more distressing
- Altered taste perception (lingual nerve)

Local anesthetic produces temporary numbness that resolves within a few hours. Numbness persisting beyond 24–48 hours may indicate nerve involvement and should be documented and reported to the surgeon.

## Pre-Operative Assessment

For lower wisdom teeth and lower posterior implants, panoramic radiography and ideally cone beam CT (CBCT) imaging are used to evaluate the relationship between root tips or planned implant depth and the inferior alveolar canal. When proximity is documented, the surgeon should discuss the specific risk, the imaging findings, and — for wisdom teeth where nerve contact is close — alternative approaches such as coronectomy (intentional partial removal, leaving roots in place near the nerve canal).

## When to Seek Further Evaluation

If altered sensation persists beyond three months without improvement, referral to an oral medicine specialist or a microsurgeon experienced in peripheral nerve repair is appropriate. Specialized sensory testing and detailed imaging can characterize the injury type and location. Interventions may include pharmacological management or, in select cases involving confirmed nerve discontinuity, microsurgical exploration and repair. Most temporary nerve injuries resolve without intervention; persistent symptoms beyond six months are less likely to resolve without further evaluation.
