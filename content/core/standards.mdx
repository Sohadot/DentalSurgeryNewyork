## Introduction

This page defines the patient decision standards referenced throughout dentalsurgerynewyork.com. These standards reflect the informational framework the site uses when presenting procedure guidance, cost context, and decision support content.

This page does not provide individualized medical advice. It does not recommend specific practitioners. Patients should discuss their clinical situation with a qualified, licensed oral surgeon or dental specialist before making any treatment decision.

---

## When to Seek a Specialist Consultation

Not every dental concern requires an oral surgeon, but several situations warrant specialist evaluation before proceeding with treatment. The following situations make independent specialist consultation appropriate to consider:

- **Impacted teeth** — Teeth that have not erupted normally, particularly third molars, often require specialist assessment before extraction decisions are made.
- **Multiple extractions or staged removal** — When more than one tooth requires removal, or removal is being planned in stages, a consultation with an oral surgeon or periodontist clarifies risk and sequencing.
- **Bone grafting preceding implants** — If an implant is planned and bone volume is insufficient, the grafting procedure is typically performed by a specialist with specific training. Confirmation of credentials and the grafting approach should occur before any extraction.
- **Sedation beyond nitrous oxide** — Patients offered IV sedation or general anesthesia should confirm that the provider holds the appropriate NYSED Anesthesia Permit and has supporting staff and monitoring equipment.
- **Systemic medical factors** — Patients with diabetes, anticoagulant use, a history of radiation to the jaw, bisphosphonate use, or significant cardiovascular history should raise these factors with any provider before surgery. A second opinion or physician consultation may be appropriate.
- **Emergency presentations** — Swelling that extends to the neck or floor of the mouth, difficulty swallowing or breathing, or high fever accompanying a dental infection are situations where emergency room evaluation should precede any elective oral surgery decision.

This list is not exhaustive. When in doubt, a second opinion from a different licensed specialist is always reasonable.

## How to Evaluate Cost Estimates

Cost estimates in oral surgery vary significantly and are frequently incomplete as initially presented. The following standards reflect how this site approaches cost information, and what patients should verify before accepting an estimate.

**Request itemized, written estimates.** Verbal quotes are not binding and frequently exclude components of treatment. A written, itemized estimate specifying each procedure code, number of implants or grafts, imaging requirements, and sedation costs is more useful for comparison.

**Ask what is not included.** The most common sources of post-treatment cost surprise are:

- Imaging (CBCT scan, panoramic X-ray) not included in quoted fees
- Sedation billed separately from the surgical fee
- Post-operative visits billed separately
- Bone graft or membrane costs excluded from implant quotes
- Crown or abutment not included in implant base price

**Understand that published ranges are directional.** Cost guides on this site present directional ranges based on publicly available information and common pricing structures in New York City. These ranges are not quotes and are not sourced from specific practices. Actual treatment costs depend on case complexity, provider pricing, and insurance status.

**Do not compare base prices without components.** A lower headline price for an implant or extraction may exclude sedation, imaging, or grafting that is included in a higher quote. Comparisons should be made on equivalent components.

**Verify insurance coverage in writing.** Benefits estimates from insurers are preliminary. Written confirmation of coverage for specific procedure codes and the expected patient share is more reliable than a verbal estimate.

## Questions Before Sedation

Before agreeing to any sedation beyond nitrous oxide for oral surgery in New York, patients should confirm the following:

1. What level of sedation is being proposed? (Minimal / Moderate / Deep / General Anesthesia)
2. Does the provider hold the appropriate NYSED Anesthesia Permit for that sedation level?
3. Who will administer and monitor sedation during the procedure?
4. What monitoring equipment is present in the operatory? (Pulse oximeter, blood pressure, EKG, capnography?)
5. What is the post-sedation discharge protocol?
6. What is the protocol if a sedation complication occurs?
7. Has the provider reviewed all current medications, supplements, and medical history for sedation contraindications?

Providers should welcome these questions. A provider who is reluctant to answer them warrants further scrutiny before proceeding.

## Emergency vs. Non-Emergency Guidance

### Seek Emergency Room or Call 911

The following presentations warrant emergency room evaluation or a 911 call — not a dental office appointment:

- Swelling that extends below the jawline, to the neck, or to the floor of the mouth
- Difficulty swallowing or breathing associated with a dental infection
- Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) with facial or oral swelling
- Altered mental status in the context of a dental infection
- Any suspected airway compromise

These are potentially life-threatening. Do not delay emergency care in favor of a dental appointment.

### Urgent Dental Situations (Same Day or Next Day)

The following situations warrant prompt contact with an oral surgeon but are not typically life-threatening:

- Severe, uncontrolled tooth pain not responding to over-the-counter analgesics
- A visible abscess limited to the gum or cheek without systemic symptoms
- A tooth fractured at the gumline after trauma
- A lost or failing dental implant with acute pain
- Post-extraction dry socket (severe pain beginning several days after extraction)

### Non-Emergency (Can Be Scheduled)

- Stable, manageable tooth sensitivity
- Planned extraction of a non-infected, non-symptomatic tooth
- Implant consultation without acute symptoms
- Routine wisdom tooth monitoring without active symptoms

This guidance is informational and does not substitute for a clinical assessment. If you are uncertain whether a situation is an emergency, err toward emergency evaluation.
