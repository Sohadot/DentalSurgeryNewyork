A dental crown is a tooth-shaped restoration placed over a prepared natural tooth or a dental implant. In oral surgery contexts, crowns appear most commonly as the final prosthetic component of a dental implant — the visible tooth portion that is cemented or screwed onto the implant abutment after osseointegration is complete.

## What a Crown Does

A crown fully covers the structure it sits on. For implants, it restores chewing function and appearance. For natural teeth that have been weakened by large decay, fracture, or root canal treatment, a crown protects the remaining tooth structure and distributes bite force.

## Crown Materials

Most dental crowns placed in New York today use one of three material categories:

- **Zirconia** — High-strength ceramic; the current standard for posterior implants and molar positions due to durability. Fully white, highly biocompatible, resists chipping better than porcelain alternatives.
- **Porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM)** — A metal substructure with a porcelain exterior layer. Older standard; now largely replaced by zirconia in implant cases, though still used in some clinical situations.
- **Lithium disilicate (e.max)** — A glass ceramic preferred for anterior aesthetic cases where translucency matters. Less suitable for high-force posterior positions on implants.

Your provider selects the material based on tooth position, bite force, adjacent teeth, and the implant system used.

## When a Crown Is Placed After Implant Surgery

Under standard implant protocol, the crown is placed approximately three to six months after implant surgery — after the implant post has integrated into the jawbone (osseointegration). An abutment is attached first, then the crown is fabricated by a dental laboratory to match adjacent teeth and secured on top.

In immediate loading protocols, a temporary crown may be placed the same day as surgery. This is the prosthetic component only — it does not mean the implant has integrated. A final crown replaces the temporary once osseointegration is confirmed at a follow-up appointment.

## Cemented vs. Screw-Retained

Implant crowns can be fixed in one of two ways:

- **Cemented**: Crown is glued to the abutment. Appearance is seamless, with no visible access hole. More difficult to retrieve if the implant needs future maintenance or removal.
- **Screw-retained**: Crown is secured with a small screw accessible through an access hole on the biting surface, covered with composite resin. Easier to remove for maintenance. The access hole is minor but visible on close inspection.

The choice is determined by implant position, angulation, and provider preference. Neither option is universally superior.

## Cost Context

In New York, a crown for a dental implant typically costs $1,500–$3,000 or more depending on material, the fabrication laboratory, and the provider. Most dental insurance plans exclude implant-supported crowns, though some plans may provide partial coverage if the crown is not classified as an implant restoration. Confirm coverage categorization with your insurance plan before treatment begins.

The crown cost is separate from the implant placement fee, abutment cost, and any imaging or grafting performed earlier in the treatment sequence. A comprehensive estimate should itemize these components individually.
