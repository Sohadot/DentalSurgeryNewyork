## Introduction

Wisdom teeth — the third molars at the back of the jaw — are the last teeth to develop, typically between the ages of 17 and 25. Many people in New York do not have enough jaw space for them to erupt properly. Whether to monitor, treat proactively, or leave wisdom teeth in place depends on their position, symptoms, and risk profile. Understanding what problems wisdom teeth can cause helps patients have a more informed conversation with a provider.

---

## Impacted vs. Erupted Wisdom Teeth

Wisdom teeth that remain fully embedded in bone or beneath the gum line are called impacted. Those that have broken through the gum surface are erupted. Problems arise from both categories, but the mechanisms differ.

**Fully impacted wisdom teeth** — May cause no symptoms for years, or may develop:
- Cysts or tumors surrounding the tooth as it attempts to develop
- Pressure-related resorption of adjacent second molars
- Pain from the follicle around the tooth

**Partially erupted wisdom teeth** — This is often the highest-risk category:
- The opening in the gum creates a pocket where food and bacteria accumulate
- Pericoronitis (infection of the gum tissue around the partially erupted tooth) can develop — causing pain, swelling, and in some cases spreading infection
- Cleaning is nearly impossible, leading to high rates of decay on the wisdom tooth and adjacent second molar

**Fully erupted wisdom teeth** — The lowest-risk category, though not risk-free:
- Cleaning access at the back of the mouth is limited; decay and gum disease risk remain elevated
- Crowding of other teeth may occur if space is insufficient
- Some erupted wisdom teeth are positioned at angles that affect the bite

---

## When Removal Is Typically Indicated

Precise indications vary by case and are assessed by imaging and clinical examination. Removal is commonly indicated when:

- Recurrent pericoronitis (two or more episodes)
- Active decay that cannot be adequately restored due to position
- Cyst or tumor formation associated with an impacted tooth
- Documented evidence of adjacent tooth resorption
- Significant crowding attributed to wisdom tooth pressure
- Pathology on imaging even without symptoms in some cases

---

## When Monitoring May Be Appropriate

Not every wisdom tooth requires removal. Fully impacted teeth with no associated pathology on imaging, no adjacent tooth impact, and no symptoms may be appropriate for periodic radiographic monitoring rather than proactive extraction. This is a clinical judgment made with imaging — not a decision that can be made based on symptoms alone.

---

## Questions to Ask Your Provider

- Has imaging been taken of my wisdom teeth, and what does it show about their position and any pathology?
- Is any cyst formation or adjacent tooth damage visible on the scan?
- Is my situation appropriate for monitoring, or do you recommend extraction — and why?
- If extraction is recommended, what is the complexity of my case and what type of anesthesia do you recommend?
