## How Do Blood Thinners Affect Oral Surgery?

Blood-thinning medications — both anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents — affect the body's ability to form clots after oral surgery. This introduces a higher risk of prolonged bleeding and requires coordination between the oral surgeon and the prescribing physician before any invasive procedure.

Patients on blood thinners should not stop or adjust their medications without direct guidance from the prescribing provider. Independent discontinuation of anticoagulant therapy carries serious risks including stroke, pulmonary embolism, or cardiac events.

## Types of Blood-Thinning Medications

### Anticoagulants
These medications interfere with the coagulation cascade — the chain of biochemical steps that produce a clot.

- **Warfarin (Coumadin)** — Managed by monitoring INR (International Normalized Ratio). Surgeons typically prefer INR below 3.5 for most oral procedures and may consult the prescribing physician for higher values.
- **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)** — Rivaroxaban (Xarelto), apixaban (Eliquis), dabigatran (Pradaxa). These have shorter half-lives than warfarin and may be managed through timing of the last dose before surgery rather than dose reduction.

### Antiplatelet Agents
These medications reduce platelet aggregation — the clumping of platelets that forms the initial plug at a bleeding site.

- **Aspirin** — Even low-dose (81 mg) aspirin significantly affects platelet function for the duration of those platelets' lifespan (7 to 10 days)
- **Clopidogrel (Plavix)** — A more potent antiplatelet agent, often prescribed after cardiac stenting
- **Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)** — Patients on aspirin plus clopidogrel have substantially increased bleeding risk and typically require cardiology consultation before elective oral surgery

## What Oral Surgeons Typically Do

For most routine extractions, oral surgeons can manage increased bleeding risk with local measures:

- Careful surgical technique to minimize tissue trauma
- Gelatin sponge, oxidized cellulose, or collagen wound plugs placed in the socket
- Sutures to close the extraction site
- Pressure gauze for an extended period after the procedure
- Instructions to avoid aspirin, NSAIDs, and alcohol during the healing period

For more complex procedures — multiple extractions, bone grafting, implant placement, or surgery under sedation — the surgeon may request a physician consultation and a recent INR value (for warfarin patients) before proceeding.

## What to Tell Your Oral Surgeon

Before any oral surgery procedure, disclose:

- The name and dose of every blood-thinning medication
- The medical condition it is prescribed for (atrial fibrillation, DVT, cardiac stenting, etc.)
- The name and contact information of the prescribing physician
- Whether you are on dual antiplatelet therapy or a combination of anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications
- The most recent INR value if you are on warfarin

## What to Ask Your Oral Surgeon and Prescribing Physician

- Should I take my blood thinner as usual on the day of the procedure, or should I skip a dose?
- Will you coordinate with my prescribing physician before deciding on an approach?
- What local hemostatic measures will you use if bleeding is prolonged?
- What signs of post-operative bleeding should I watch for at home, and when should I call?

**Never stop blood-thinning medications without explicit instruction from your prescribing physician.** Oral surgeons are experienced in managing increased bleeding risk with local measures; the risk of stopping necessary anticoagulation almost always outweighs the added bleeding risk of continuing it through a routine dental procedure.
