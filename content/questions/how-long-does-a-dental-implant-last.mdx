## How Long Does a Dental Implant Last?

Dental implants are often described as a permanent or lifetime solution, but this framing conflates two distinct components: the implant post and the crown. Understanding what each part is expected to do, and what can cause either to fail, gives patients a more accurate picture of what they are committing to.

## The Implant Post

The titanium implant post — the screw-shaped fixture anchored into the jawbone — is the most durable component of the system. When osseointegration is successful and ongoing maintenance prevents peri-implant disease, implant posts have demonstrated survival rates of 90 to 95 percent or higher at the 10-year mark in long-term clinical follow-up studies. Many implants placed in the 1960s and 1970s are still functioning today.

This does not mean implant posts never fail. Early failure (within the first year) relates to failed osseointegration. Late failure (years or decades after placement) typically involves peri-implantitis — bacterial infection causing bone loss around the implant — that progresses to the point where the implant loses its structural support.

## The Crown

The crown placed over the implant — the visible tooth-shaped restoration — has a shorter expected lifespan than the implant post itself. Implant crowns are typically expected to last **10 to 15 years** under normal function before requiring replacement due to wear, chipping, or changes in the surrounding gum architecture. Ceramic and zirconia crowns are more wear-resistant than older porcelain materials.

An implant post that remains well-integrated can often receive a replacement crown without further surgery.

## What Affects Implant Longevity

Factors that influence how long an implant lasts:

- **Oral hygiene** — The single most controllable variable. Peri-implant mucositis (reversible gum inflammation) progresses to peri-implantitis (bone loss) when plaque is not managed consistently
- **Smoking** — Reduces blood supply to the gum and bone, slows healing, and significantly increases the rate of peri-implantitis
- **Systemic conditions** — Poorly controlled diabetes, osteoporosis, and certain medications affecting bone metabolism can reduce implant survival rates
- **Grinding and clenching** — Bruxism places sustained off-axis load on the implant and crown, accelerating wear and potentially causing implant or crown fracture
- **Bone quality at placement** — Dense bone (Type 1 or 2) provides more stable integration than low-density bone; grafted bone may integrate differently than native bone
- **Surgical technique and implant brand** — These are harder for patients to assess independently, but board certification and case volume are proxies worth asking about

## Maintenance After Implant Placement

Implant maintenance is not the same as standard tooth brushing. Key practices:

- Professional cleaning at a frequency recommended by your provider (often every 3 to 4 months for the first year, then twice yearly if stable)
- Interdental brushes or water flossers to clean around the implant neck, where a standard toothbrush cannot effectively reach
- Periodic probing and radiographic monitoring to detect early peri-implant changes before they progress

An implant that is well-maintained and monitored is far more likely to remain functional for decades than one that receives no specialized follow-up.

## Questions to Ask Before Treatment

- What implant system are you using, and what are its published survival rates at 10 years?
- What maintenance protocol do you recommend after placement?
- What would indicate that the implant is failing, and what would the response be?
- How does my medical history (diabetes, smoking, bone density) affect expected longevity in my case?
