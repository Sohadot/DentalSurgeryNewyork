## Introduction

Not every dental problem is an emergency, but some oral conditions escalate quickly and require prompt evaluation. Understanding the difference between an urgent situation and a routine one — and knowing where to go — matters in New York, where access and options vary by borough, time of day, and the nature of the problem.

This guide is intended to help patients recognize concerning signs, understand when emergency evaluation is appropriate, and know what to expect in an urgent oral surgery context.

## This Page Is Not a Substitute for Emergency Care

If you are experiencing difficulty breathing, significant difficulty swallowing, severe spreading facial swelling, or other symptoms suggesting a systemic emergency, contact emergency services or go to the nearest emergency room immediately.

This guide addresses the landscape of urgent oral surgery evaluation — not acute medical emergencies.

## What Makes a Dental Situation Urgent

Some oral surgery situations require same-day or next-day evaluation. Signs that a situation may be urgent include:

- **Significant swelling** that is spreading beyond the tooth or gum into the face, jaw, or neck
- **Fever** alongside dental pain or swelling
- **Difficulty opening the mouth** (trismus)
- **Pulsating or rapidly worsening pain** that does not respond to over-the-counter medication
- **Trauma** to the face, jaw, or teeth following an accident or injury
- **A tooth that has been knocked out (avulsed)** — time is a critical factor in reimplantation possibility
- **Increasing pain after extraction** that worsens rather than improves after 3–4 days (possible dry socket)
- **Swelling or pain after a recent procedure** that seems disproportionate or worsening

Not all of these require an emergency room. Many require prompt contact with an oral surgeon or dental provider.

## Dental Infection: Why It Can Escalate

Dental infections — whether from an untreated cavity, a cracked tooth, a failed root canal, or a partially erupted wisdom tooth — can spread beyond the tooth itself. Bacterial spread to surrounding soft tissue spaces in the jaw, face, and neck can create conditions that compromise breathing or become life-threatening.

Swelling that extends below the jaw, into the floor of the mouth, or causes difficulty swallowing or breathing is a medical emergency that should be evaluated in an emergency room, not a dental office.

Pain alone, without these signs, may still require prompt attention but is less likely to be a true medical emergency.

## When to Go to an Emergency Room vs. an Oral Surgeon

**Go to an emergency room when:**
- swelling is spreading rapidly to the jaw, neck, or floor of the mouth
- there is difficulty breathing or swallowing
- there is a high fever accompanying significant swelling
- there has been significant trauma with concern about fracture, airway, or blood loss

**Contact an oral surgeon directly when:**
- there is significant dental pain, a suspected abscess, or swelling confined to the gum or face near the tooth
- a tooth has been knocked out and a provider can be reached within a reasonable timeframe
- you are experiencing post-operative pain or complications after a recent procedure
- a partially erupted wisdom tooth is acutely inflamed

In New York, many oral surgery practices have protocols for urgent same-day appointments. Contacting a provider directly — or asking your general dentist for a referral — is often the fastest path to appropriate care.

## Avulsed (Knocked-Out) Teeth

When a permanent tooth is completely knocked out, timing affects whether reimplantation is possible. General guidance:

- Handle the tooth by the crown, not the root surface
- Do not scrub the root surface
- If possible, keep the tooth moist — either repositioned in the socket, held in the mouth between the cheek and gum, or placed in cold milk
- Seek oral surgery evaluation as soon as possible

This guidance applies to adult permanent teeth. Primary (baby) teeth are not typically reimplanted.

## Post-Procedure Complications to Watch For

Patients recovering from oral surgery should be aware of warning signs that warrant a call to their provider:

- pain that significantly worsens after day 3–4 of recovery
- swelling that increases after the first 48 hours rather than decreasing
- fever above 101°F (38.3°C)
- unusual bleeding that does not respond to pressure
- numbness or sensation changes that persist after the initial anesthesia has worn off

These situations should be reported to the treating provider, not managed with additional medication without guidance.

## Cost and Access Considerations in New York

Emergency oral surgery in New York involves cost complexity:

- after-hours or same-day appointment fees vary significantly between practices
- emergency room treatment typically addresses infection with medication but may not extract the tooth — a referral to an oral surgeon often follows
- some emergency situations require imaging billed separately
- sedation for an emergency extraction may be recommended and may not be included in the initial quoted cost

Patients should ask whether the emergency fee includes imaging and follow-up, and what happens if treatment beyond the initial visit is required.

## Questions to Ask in an Urgent Situation

When contacting a provider in an urgent situation:

- Can you see me today or tomorrow?
- Is this something that requires emergency room evaluation first?
- What are the signs I should go to the emergency room rather than wait?
- If I come in, what will the visit include and what does it cost?
- Will the procedure require sedation, and what does that add?
- What aftercare will I need, and will medication be prescribed?
