## Overview

Smoking is one of the most commonly discussed modifiable risk factors for dental implant outcomes. Its effects operate through multiple mechanisms that can affect every phase of the implant treatment sequence — from healing after extraction through osseointegration and long-term implant maintenance.

This page describes the mechanisms involved, the categories of risk, and what questions a patient who smokes should ask before and during implant treatment.

## How Smoking Affects Healing After Extraction

Before an implant can be placed, the extraction site needs to heal. Smoking impairs this healing in several ways:

- Nicotine reduces blood flow to soft tissue and bone, slowing the initial healing response
- Carbon monoxide reduces oxygen availability in the blood
- Heat and chemical exposure disrupt the protective clot that forms in the extraction socket
- Dry socket risk is significantly higher in patients who smoke, particularly in the first 72 hours

If socket preservation (bone grafting at the time of extraction) is planned, smoking increases the risk of graft failure or incomplete integration during the healing period.

## How Smoking Affects Osseointegration

Osseointegration — the process by which bone grows around and bonds to the implant surface — is the critical biological requirement for implant success. Smoking interferes with this process through:

- reduced bone density and slower bone remodeling activity
- compromised immune response at the surgical site
- reduced blood supply to the peri-implant tissue
- higher risk of early implant failure during the integration period

The implant failure rate in smokers is consistently higher than in non-smokers across the implant literature. The degree of risk correlates with quantity smoked and whether the patient stops smoking around the procedure.

## How Smoking Affects Long-Term Implant Health

Even after successful osseointegration, smoking affects long-term implant health:

- Peri-implantitis — infection around the implant that leads to bone loss — is more common in smokers
- Gum tissue around implants heals differently in smokers, with less vascular response
- Bone loss around implant sites progresses faster in smokers over time

## What Providers Typically Discuss

Most providers who place implants will discuss smoking status as part of the pre-treatment assessment. Some providers advise cessation before and after surgery as a standard protocol requirement. Others may advise reducing use or timing cessation around the procedure.

Patients who smoke should expect this conversation and should ask:

- How does my smoking history affect my candidacy for implants?
- Is there a cessation protocol I should follow before or after the procedure?
- How does this change the expected timeline or outcome?
- Are there additional monitoring requirements given my smoking history?
