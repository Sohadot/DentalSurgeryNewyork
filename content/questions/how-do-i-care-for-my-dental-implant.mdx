Dental implants do not decay the way natural teeth do — the titanium post and ceramic crown are not susceptible to cavities — but they can fail through a different mechanism: peri-implantitis, a bacteria-driven inflammatory condition that destroys the bone around the implant over time. Long-term implant success depends on daily plaque control and regular professional maintenance.

## Daily Oral Hygiene

**Brushing.** Brush twice daily with a soft-bristled toothbrush. Electric toothbrushes are effective. Use low-abrasion toothpaste; highly abrasive formulas can scratch the crown surface over time.

**Interdental cleaning.** The space between the implant crown and adjacent teeth — and the space under the crown where it meets the gum — must be cleaned daily. Options include:
- **Interdental brushes** (proxabrush) sized to fit the space: preferred by most implant clinicians
- **Water flossers**: effective at flushing bacteria from the gum margin around implants
- **Implant-specific floss or tape**: designed to clean under and around the crown without catching on the subgingival components

Standard floss with a threading motion can work but may not reach the deeper sulcus around implants as effectively as the options above.

## Professional Maintenance

Schedule professional cleaning at least every six months. Inform your dental hygienist that you have implants — implant surfaces require non-metal instruments (plastic or titanium scalers) to avoid scratching the post surface below the gum line. Metal scalers can cause surface damage that promotes bacterial adhesion and accelerates peri-implant disease.

The dentist or implant provider should periodically evaluate the implant with:
- Probing depths around the implant (comparable to periodontal probing around natural teeth)
- Radiographs to assess bone level at the implant interface (typically at the one-year mark and then every two to three years if stable)

## Early Warning Signs to Report

Contact your provider if you notice:
- Bleeding around the implant when brushing (may indicate early peri-implant mucositis)
- Pain or discomfort at the implant site
- Any mobility or looseness in the crown or implant
- A visible gap between the gum and crown base
- Persistent bad taste or foul odor localized to the implant area

Early peri-implant mucositis (inflammation limited to the soft tissue) is reversible with professional treatment and improved home care. If it progresses to peri-implantitis with bone loss, treatment becomes more complex and outcomes less predictable.

## Crown Longevity

The implant post can last decades under appropriate conditions. The crown placed on top has a shorter functional life — typically ten to fifteen years depending on material, bite force, and hygiene. A screw-retained crown is easier to remove and replace than a cemented one when the time comes. Budget for eventual crown replacement as part of long-term implant planning.

## Lifestyle Factors

- **Smoking**: accelerates peri-implant bone loss and increases risk of peri-implantitis. Cessation significantly improves long-term implant outcomes.
- **Uncontrolled diabetes**: impairs healing and immune response. Maintaining HbA1c in the acceptable range reduces long-term implant risk.
- **Bruxism (teeth grinding)**: generates mechanical overload on implants. A night guard reduces this risk if grinding is documented.
- **Hard foods**: avoid habitually biting very hard items (ice, hard candies, bones) with the implant crown.
