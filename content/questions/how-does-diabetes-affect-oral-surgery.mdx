## How Does Diabetes Affect Oral Surgery?

Diabetes affects multiple aspects of oral surgery outcomes — including healing speed, infection risk, and the success of osseointegration for dental implants. It is not a disqualifier for most oral surgery procedures, but it is a significant risk modifier that requires documentation, disclosure, and in some cases pre-surgical management.

## Healing and Infection Risk

Elevated blood glucose levels impair the body's ability to heal wounds and fight infection. Specifically:

- **Slower wound healing** — High glucose levels reduce white blood cell function and collagen synthesis, both critical to tissue repair after oral surgery
- **Higher infection risk** — Diabetic patients have a higher rate of post-surgical infections, including at extraction sites and implant placements
- **Dry socket** — Some evidence suggests elevated risk of alveolar osteitis (dry socket) after tooth extraction in patients with poor glycemic control
- **Delayed osseointegration** — For dental implants, the bone-implant integration process may be slower or less complete in patients with elevated HbA1c

## HbA1c and Oral Surgery

HbA1c (hemoglobin A1c) reflects average blood glucose levels over the prior 2–3 months. Oral surgeons may review a patient's HbA1c before elective procedures. General reference points from the surgical literature:

- HbA1c below 7–8% is typically considered acceptable for most elective oral surgery
- HbA1c above 9–10% may prompt a surgeon to delay elective procedures until glycemic control improves

These are general reference points, not universal thresholds. Individual surgeon judgment, the nature of the procedure, and other patient factors all affect the decision.

## What to Disclose Before Surgery

Patients with diabetes should disclose the following to their oral surgeon before any procedure:

- Type of diabetes (Type 1, Type 2, or other)
- Current medications, including insulin and oral hypoglycemics
- Most recent HbA1c value and the date it was measured
- Any recent episodes of significant hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia
- Contact information for the treating endocrinologist or primary care physician

Surgeons may request a letter from the treating physician or a recent lab report before proceeding with complex elective cases.

## Fasting and Medications on the Day of Surgery

For patients taking insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents, fasting before sedation or general anesthesia requires careful coordination. Going without food while continuing normal medication doses can cause dangerous drops in blood sugar during the procedure.

Before the procedure, ask your surgeon's team:

- Should I take my diabetes medications on the morning of surgery?
- Should I adjust my insulin dose on the day of the procedure?
- What is the protocol if my blood sugar is out of range on the day of surgery?

These questions should be answered with input from your endocrinologist or primary care provider, not the surgical team alone.

## Dental Implants and Diabetes

Dental implants are not contraindicated for most well-controlled diabetic patients. Evidence supports acceptable implant survival rates in patients with HbA1c below 8%, though long-term maintenance is more important for this population — including more frequent monitoring for peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis, both of which have higher prevalence in diabetic patients.

Poorly controlled diabetes significantly elevates implant failure risk. Surgical teams will typically want documented evidence of current glycemic management before proceeding with an implant plan.

## Questions to Ask Your Oral Surgeon

- Given my diabetes history, what is your threshold for proceeding with this procedure?
- Should I get updated lab work before we schedule?
- What signs of post-operative infection or delayed healing should I watch for?
- Will my diabetes affect the osseointegration timeline for implants?
- How does your team coordinate with my primary care physician or endocrinologist for complex procedures?
