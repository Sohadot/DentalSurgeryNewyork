## What This Page Is

This page defines the conceptual framework used throughout this site. It maps the core terms, decision nodes, and procedural relationships that structure oral surgery as a patient-facing category. Its purpose is to make the knowledge model explicit — not just to define words, but to show how concepts relate to each other and to patient decisions.

This is not a clinical reference. It is an informational taxonomy designed to help patients navigate a category where terminology is often used inconsistently by providers, insurers, and advertisers.

---

## Domain: Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (Oral Surgery)

Oral surgery is the specialty concerned with surgical procedures involving the teeth, jaw, and surrounding tissues. As a patient-facing category, it spans:

- **Dentoalveolar surgery** — surgical management of teeth and the alveolar bone that supports them (extractions, socket preservation, implant placement)
- **Preprosthetic surgery** — surgical preparation of the jaw for prosthetic devices, including bone grafting and sinus lifts
- **Implant surgery** — placement of titanium root-form implants into the jawbone as a foundation for prosthetic teeth
- **Emergency oral surgery** — urgent management of dental infection, acute pain, or traumatic injury

---

## Tier 1: Foundational Concepts

These are the base-level concepts on which all procedural and cost decisions depend.

### Alveolar Bone
The bone that surrounds and supports tooth roots. When teeth are removed, alveolar bone undergoes resorption — a natural process of diminishment. The rate and extent of resorption varies by patient. Alveolar bone volume is the primary limiting factor for dental implant placement.

### Osseointegration
The biological process by which bone grows onto and bonds with the surface of a titanium implant. Osseointegration is required for implant success. Failure of osseointegration is the primary mechanism of early implant failure. The process typically requires several months.

### Dental Occlusion
The relationship between upper and lower teeth when the jaw is closed. Occlusion affects how forces are distributed across teeth and implants. Missing teeth disrupt occlusion through drift of adjacent teeth and over-eruption of opposing teeth.

### Anesthesia Continuum
A spectrum of sedation states: (1) local anesthesia — site-specific numbing, patient fully conscious; (2) minimal sedation — anxiolytic medication, patient responsive; (3) moderate sedation / conscious sedation — patient may be unaware of surroundings but responsive to stimulation; (4) deep sedation — patient not easily aroused; (5) general anesthesia — patient fully unconscious. In New York, providers who administer Level 3 or above in outpatient settings require specific permits from the New York State Education Department.

## Tier 2: Procedure Concepts

Procedure concepts describe the surgical interventions covered by this reference and their relationships to each other.

### Tooth Extraction
**Definition:** Surgical or non-surgical removal of a tooth from its socket in the alveolar bone.

**Subtypes:**
- *Simple extraction* — removal of an erupted tooth with forceps and elevator instruments
- *Surgical extraction* — removal requiring an incision, bone removal, or tooth sectioning (common for impacted teeth or fractured roots)

**Downstream relationships:**
- Extraction creates a socket. Without intervention, the socket undergoes bone resorption.
- Extraction may be followed by socket preservation to protect future implant eligibility.
- Extraction is often the first step in an implant treatment sequence.

### Wisdom Tooth Removal
**Definition:** Extraction of the third molars (wisdom teeth), which may be fully erupted, partially erupted, or impacted.

**Impaction classification:**
- *Soft tissue impaction* — tooth has emerged through bone but is partially covered by gum tissue
- *Partial bony impaction* — tooth is partially covered by bone
- *Complete bony impaction* — tooth is entirely enclosed in bone

**Relationship to extraction:** Wisdom tooth removal is a subtype of surgical extraction. Impaction severity is the primary driver of surgical complexity, sedation requirements, and cost.

### Socket Preservation
**Definition:** A bone grafting procedure performed at the time of tooth extraction that fills the empty socket with grafting material to slow post-extraction bone resorption.

**When it applies:** Relevant when future implant placement is anticipated at the extraction site.

**Materials:** Autograft (patient's own bone), allograft (donor bone), xenograft (animal-derived), or synthetic substitutes. The choice of material affects both procedure cost and integration timeline.

**Relationship to implants:** Socket preservation is a precursor intervention that increases the likelihood that adequate bone volume exists when implant placement is planned.

### Bone Grafting
**Definition:** A category of surgical procedures that augment bone volume at the jaw using graft material. Socket preservation is one type. Other types include:

- *Ridge augmentation (block or particulate)* — lateral expansion of the jaw ridge for implant placement
- *Sinus lift (sinus augmentation)* — elevation of the sinus membrane to allow placement of bone in the upper jaw posterior region
- *GBR (guided bone regeneration)* — use of a membrane barrier to direct bone growth into a targeted area

**Relationships:** Bone grafting is a prerequisite procedure when bone volume is insufficient for implant placement. The need for grafting is determined by imaging (typically cone beam CT).

### Dental Implant Placement
**Definition:** Surgical insertion of a titanium root-form implant into the alveolar bone to serve as an anchor for a dental prosthesis (crown, bridge, or denture).

**Component structure:**
- *Implant body* — the titanium fixture placed in the bone (placed by surgeon)
- *Abutment* — the connector between the implant body and the prosthetic crown
- *Crown/prosthesis* — the visible tooth replacement (placed by restorative dentist or prosthodontist)

**Clinical sequence:**
1. Bone assessment (imaging) →
2. Extraction if needed →
3. Socket preservation or bone grafting if needed →
4. Integration/healing period →
5. Implant placement →
6. Osseointegration period →
7. Abutment placement →
8. Final crown delivery

### Emergency Oral Surgery
**Definition:** Urgent surgical intervention for conditions that cannot wait for a routine appointment. Includes drainage of dental abscess, management of severe acute pulpitis, treatment of traumatic dental injury, or removal of acutely infected teeth.

**Triage decision node:** Not all urgent dental situations require an oral surgeon. Routine toothache or broken teeth may be managed by a general dentist. Signs of spreading infection (facial swelling, trismus, difficulty breathing or swallowing, fever) require immediate evaluation and may require emergency room care.

## Tier 3: Cost Concepts

Cost concepts describe the structure of pricing in oral surgery and the factors that cause variability.

### Treatment Episode vs. Component Billing
Oral surgery treatment is almost always billed by component, not as a single episode. An implant quote that reads “$3,000” may include only the implant body and may exclude imaging, sedation, extraction, socket preservation, abutment, and crown. A complete treatment episode for a single implant may involve multiple providers, multiple insurance policy types, and multiple billing events.

### Sedation Billing Structure
Sedation in oral surgery is typically billed separately from the surgical fee. Common billing structures: (1) flat fee per procedure regardless of duration; (2) time-based fee in 15-minute increments. Sedation fees vary by type — nitrous oxide is less expensive than IV sedation, which is less expensive than general anesthesia administered by a separate anesthesiologist.

### Directional Range vs. Quote
A directional range is an informational reference point based on observable market data. It is not a quote. A quote is a provider-specific fee for a specific scope of treatment in a specific patient. This site publishes directional ranges, not quotes.

## Tier 4: Provider and Credential Concepts

### Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeon (OMFS)
A dental specialist who has completed dental school followed by a four-to-six year hospital-based surgical residency. OMFS training includes both dental and medical surgery, anesthesia administration, and management of complex surgical cases. In New York, OMFS practitioners hold a dental license with a specialty registration.

### Credential Hierarchy for Anesthesia
New York State requires specific permits for outpatient sedation beyond local anesthesia. Permit types reflect the level of sedation the provider is authorized to administer. Patients should confirm which permit level a provider holds and whether it matches the planned sedation type.

### Board Certification
Oral surgeons may seek board certification from the American Board of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (ABOMS). Certification requires completion of an approved residency, passage of written and oral examinations, and active maintenance. Board certification is voluntary, not required to practice.

## Tier 5: Insurance and Coverage Concepts

### Dental vs. Medical Benefit
Oral surgery may be covered under a dental insurance plan, a medical insurance plan, or both, depending on the procedure and how the claim is categorized. Dental plans typically have annual maximums. Medical plans apply when medical necessity criteria are met. Many patients have both benefit types and can file against each for eligible procedures.

### Prior Authorization
A process by which an insurer reviews and pre-approves a proposed procedure before treatment occurs. Not all procedures require prior authorization, but many insurers require it for implants, bone grafting, and complex surgical procedures. Treatment rendered without required prior authorization may not be covered.

### Predetermination of Benefits
A written statement from an insurer estimating the coverage applicable to a proposed course of treatment. Not a guarantee of payment, but the closest approximation available before treatment. Patients should request predetermination for any complex or costly procedure before scheduling.

## Concept Relationships: Decision Chain Model

The following represents the most common patient decision chain in this category:

```
Tooth Loss or Planned Extraction
         ↓
Bone Volume Assessment (imaging required)
         ↓
     Is bone adequate?
    /              \
  Yes              No
   ↓                ↓
Implant         Socket Preservation
Placement       or Bone Grafting
Planning             ↓
                Healing Period
                     ↓
               Bone Re-assessment
                     ↓
               Implant Placement
```

The decision at the bone volume node is the primary driver of treatment complexity, timeline, and cost. Patients who are not informed of this decision node may receive quotes that do not reflect the full scope of treatment their anatomy requires.
