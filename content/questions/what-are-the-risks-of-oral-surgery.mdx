## Overview

Every oral surgery procedure carries some degree of risk, and understanding that risk structure is part of informed consent. Risk does not mean a procedure should be avoided — it means that a patient should understand what is possible, what factors influence likelihood, and what questions to ask before agreeing to treatment.

This page describes the most commonly discussed risk categories across oral surgery procedures and the factors that affect how risk is understood and managed in a clinical context.

## Common Risk Categories in Oral Surgery

### Bleeding

Some degree of bleeding is expected after most oral surgery procedures. The relevant questions are about whether bleeding is controlled during the procedure, how much is expected afterward, and what constitutes a reason to contact the provider. Patients on blood thinners or with clotting conditions should inform their provider before treatment.

### Infection

Post-operative infection is one of the more common complications in oral surgery. Signs may include increasing swelling or pain after the expected early recovery period, discharge, or fever. Most cases resolve with antibiotics when caught early. Infection risk is influenced by procedural complexity, oral hygiene conditions, pre-existing health factors, and how well post-operative care instructions are followed.

### Dry Socket

Dry socket — the loss of the blood clot from an extraction site — is specific to tooth extraction and wisdom tooth removal. It typically presents as increasing pain after the first few days, often radiating to the jaw or ear. It is not an infection but requires provider management. Risk is influenced by smoking, oral rinses, and the complexity of the extraction.

### Nerve Involvement

Some procedures — particularly lower wisdom tooth removal, dental implant placement, and bone grafting in the mandible — carry a risk of temporary or, in rare cases, prolonged changes in sensation in the lip, chin, cheek, or tongue. This reflects proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve or its branches. Imaging, including CBCT scans, allows providers to assess this before treatment. The risk level should be part of the pre-treatment conversation.

### Anesthesia and Sedation Response

Reactions to sedation or anesthesia are uncommon but possible. Risk is influenced by the type of sedation used, the provider's credentials and monitoring protocols, and the patient's health history. Patients should disclose all medications, supplements, and health conditions before any procedure involving sedation.

### Implant and Graft Failure

For implant and grafting procedures specifically, failure to integrate or failure of the graft material is a recognized risk category. Early implant failure typically occurs during the osseointegration period. Late failure is often associated with infection (peri-implantitis) or mechanical factors. Graft failure may require the procedure to be staged differently or repeated.

## Factors That Influence Risk

Risk level is not fixed. It is affected by:

- the complexity of the specific procedure
- the patient's systemic health, medications, and history
- the credentials and experience of the provider
- the type of facility and monitoring available
- how thoroughly pre-operative assessment was conducted
- how completely the patient follows pre- and post-operative instructions

## Questions to Ask Before Treatment

- What are the most likely complications for this specific procedure in my case?
- How do my health conditions or medications affect that risk?
- What monitoring is in place during and after the procedure?
- What would indicate a complication I should report, and to whom?
- What is the contingency if a complication occurs?
