## Introduction

Bone resorption is the process by which jawbone diminishes after a tooth is lost. It is one of the most significant long-term consequences of tooth loss — and a key factor in determining whether dental implants are possible without grafting, and what the grafting requirements look like when they are. Understanding what bone resorption is and why it occurs helps New York patients make better decisions about the timing of tooth replacement.

---

## What Bone Resorption Is

The alveolar bone — the portion of the jawbone that surrounds and supports tooth roots — is maintained by functional stimulation. When a tooth is present and in use, chewing forces are transmitted through the root to the surrounding bone, signaling the body to maintain bone density and volume at that site.

When a tooth is removed and not replaced, that functional stimulation is gone. The body interprets the absence of load as a signal that bone at that location is no longer needed. Osteoclast activity (bone breakdown) exceeds osteoblast activity (bone formation), and the bone gradually resorbs — shrinking in both width and height.

---

## How Fast Bone Loss Occurs

Bone resorption after tooth extraction is not immediate, but it begins quickly:

- **First 6 months**: The most rapid phase of bone loss. Up to 25% of alveolar bone width may be lost in the first year, with the majority occurring in the first 6 months.
- **Ongoing**: Resorption continues at a slower rate for years afterward. Long-term denture wearers often have significantly reduced bone volume after a decade or more.
- **Vertical vs. horizontal loss**: Both width and height of the ridge diminish. Height loss affects neighboring teeth's support; width loss affects implant feasibility.

---

## How Bone Resorption Affects Implant Candidacy

Dental implants require sufficient bone volume at the implant site to be placed successfully. The minimum bone width and height for a standard implant is a threshold below which the implant cannot be placed without grafting.

If significant resorption has occurred:

- **Moderate bone loss**: Ridge augmentation or socket preservation-derived bone may restore sufficient volume for implant placement
- **Significant bone loss**: Major grafting — including sinus lifts for upper posterior sites — may be required, adding cost, complexity, and treatment time
- **Severe, long-term resorption**: Some cases may not support implants even with grafting, and alternative prosthetic approaches may be more appropriate

This is why the timing of implant consideration matters. The longer a tooth has been missing without a socket preservation or bone graft, the more bone volume may have been lost.

---

## How Bone Resorption Is Prevented

**Socket preservation** at the time of extraction places bone material in the socket to prevent or minimize the resorption that would otherwise occur. It is not always required, but it is most commonly recommended when future implant placement at the same site is planned.

For patients with existing bone loss, ridge augmentation adds bone to restore volume before implant placement. For upper posterior sites, a sinus lift may be required to address bone loss beneath the sinus floor.

---

## Questions to Ask When a Tooth Is Being Removed

- How much bone loss will occur if I do not proceed with socket preservation?
- Is socket preservation recommended at this site, and what does it add to the overall cost and timeline?
- If I decide to pursue an implant later, what is the likely bone status at that point?
