## How to Use This Compass

This Decision Compass is a structured reference framework. It helps you identify which questions are relevant to your situation and what to bring to an oral surgeon consultation. It does not constitute a diagnosis or clinical recommendation.

Work through the decision path that matches your starting point. Each path ends at an action point — a specific question or step to take before or during a provider consultation.

---

## Decision Path 1: Am I a Candidate for Dental Implants?

**Starting point:** You have a missing tooth, or a tooth that needs to be removed and replaced.

**Question 1: Has bone volume at the intended implant site been assessed with imaging?**

- **No imaging yet** — A CBCT cone beam scan is the necessary first step. Implant candidacy cannot be determined from visual examination or X-ray alone. Request imaging before accepting or rejecting any candidacy assessment.
- **Imaging has been done** — Continue to Question 2.

**Question 2: What did the imaging show about bone volume at the site?**

- **Sufficient bone volume confirmed** — Continue to Question 3.
- **Insufficient bone volume** — Bone grafting may be required before implant placement. Move to Decision Path 2.
- **Not discussed or unclear** — Ask your provider to explain the specific bone volume measurements and what they mean. A candidacy determination without imaging interpretation is incomplete.

**Question 3: Is there active infection, untreated gum disease, or unhealed tissue at the intended site?**

- **Yes** — The condition must be resolved before implant placement. Timeline and effect on long-term candidacy depends on the type and extent of the issue.
- **No** — From a bone volume and tissue standpoint, implant candidacy is likely. Proceed to treatment planning consultation.

**Action point:** Ask your provider in writing: "What specific bone volume measurement was recorded at the implant site, and what does that mean for candidacy?"

---

## Decision Path 2: Do I Need a Bone Graft Before Dental Implants?

**Starting point:** You are planning dental implants and bone loss is a question or concern.

**Question 1: Has a CBCT scan specifically assessed bone volume at the planned implant site?**

- **No** — This is the necessary starting point. Bone graft need cannot be determined without imaging. Request a CBCT scan before accepting any treatment recommendation.
- **Yes** — Continue to Question 2.

**Question 2: What did imaging show about bone volume?**

- **Sufficient bone for implant placement** — No graft required for implant candidacy at this site. Note: if a tooth is being extracted simultaneously, socket preservation may still be discussed as a preventive measure.
- **Moderate bone loss at the socket or ridge** — Socket preservation or ridge augmentation may be indicated. Ask: what graft material will be used, will a membrane be placed, and what is the timing before implant placement?
- **Significant bone loss, particularly in the upper posterior jaw** — A sinus lift may be required due to proximity of the maxillary sinus. Ask: is this an internal or external sinus lift, and will it be staged separately or simultaneous with implant placement?

**Action point:** Confirm imaging review has occurred. Ask specifically: "What is the bone volume measurement at the implant site, what graft approach do you recommend, and why?"

For background: [Bone Grafting in New York](/procedures/bone-grafting) and [Is Bone Grafting Required for Dental Implants?](/questions/is-bone-grafting-required-for-dental-implants)

## Decision Path 3: What Sedation Level Is Appropriate for My Procedure?

**Starting point:** You are scheduled for oral surgery and need to understand sedation options.

**Question 1: What is the procedure?**

- **Simple extraction of a visible, single-rooted tooth** — Local anesthesia is typically sufficient. Sedation is elective and primarily used for anxiety management in this context, not surgical necessity.
- **Surgical extraction of an impacted or multi-rooted tooth** — Conscious sedation (IV or oral) is commonly offered. General anesthesia is available for complex cases or patient preference.
- **Multiple extractions in a single session** — IV sedation or general anesthesia is commonly offered. Ask who administers the sedation — the oral surgeon or a separate anesthesiologist.
- **Bone grafting, implant placement, or sinus lift** — IV sedation or general anesthesia is standard. Confirm anesthesia provider credentials — either an OMFS with formal anesthesia training or a board-certified anesthesiologist.

**Question 2: Who is administering the sedation?**

- **The oral surgeon** — Ask about their specific anesthesia training and what monitoring equipment will be used during the procedure.
- **A separate anesthesiologist or CRNA** — Ask about their credentials, whether they are board-certified, and how the anesthesia fee will appear on billing.
- **Not yet determined or not discussed** — Clarify this before scheduling. Sedation provider identity affects credentials, billing, monitoring, and consent structure.

**Action point:** Ask before scheduling: "Who specifically will administer sedation, what are their credentials, and will there be a separate anesthesia consent and billing line?"

For background: [Oral Surgery Sedation in New York](/procedures/oral-surgery-sedation) and [Anesthesia Credentials Explained](/guides/anesthesia-credentials-explained)

## Decision Path 4: Is My Situation an Emergency?

**Starting point:** You have acute pain, swelling, or an injury and are unsure of the urgency.

**Question 1: Do you have any of the following symptoms?**

- Rapidly spreading facial or neck swelling
- Difficulty swallowing or breathing
- Fever combined with dental pain or swelling
- Inability to fully open your mouth accompanied by swelling

If yes to any of the above: **go to a hospital emergency department immediately.** These are signs of spreading infection — such as cellulitis or Ludwig's angina — that require systemic medical intervention beyond the scope of an outpatient oral surgery office.

**Question 2: Is pain severe and uncontrolled with standard over-the-counter medication?**

- **Yes** — Contact an oral surgeon's office directly. Most New York oral surgery practices have after-hours lines. Same-day emergency appointments are often available.
- **No** — Continue to Question 3.

**Question 3: What is the specific presenting situation?**

- **Broken tooth with sharp edges or exposed nerve** — Urgent dental appointment within 24 hours.
- **Lost crown or filling, no acute pain** — Schedule within a few days. Not a surgical emergency.
- **Dull persistent ache or sensitivity** — Schedule a routine appointment. Bring imaging if available.
- **Localized swelling not spreading, manageable pain** — Contact a provider during business hours for same-day or next-day evaluation.

**Action point:** When in doubt, call an oral surgeon's office and describe your symptoms. They can advise on urgency. See: [Emergency Oral Surgery in New York](/procedures/emergency-oral-surgery)

## Decision Path 5: Should I Seek a Second Opinion?

**Starting point:** You have received a treatment recommendation and want to evaluate whether to seek another assessment.

**Question 1: Was the treatment recommendation supported by imaging you have reviewed?**

- **No imaging, or imaging not shared with you** — Request your imaging records. Patients are entitled to copies of their own records in New York. A recommendation made without shared imaging review warrants verification.
- **Yes, imaging was reviewed and discussed** — Continue to Question 2.

**Question 2: Does the recommended treatment involve any of the following?**

- Bone grafting or sinus lift
- Multiple implants or full-arch implant treatment
- Unusual staging or sequencing not clearly explained
- A total treatment cost significantly exceeding what you anticipated
- Any procedure whose necessity was not clearly explained

If yes to any of the above: a second opinion from a different oral surgeon is appropriate and commonly sought for complex or high-cost treatment plans.

**Action point:** Use [Questions to Ask Before Oral Surgery](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-surgery) at both consultations. Compare not just cost, but treatment rationale, imaging interpretation, and proposed sequencing.

---

## What This Compass Does Not Replace

This framework cannot assess your specific anatomy, imaging, medical history, or case complexity. It is designed to help you arrive at provider consultations with better questions and a clearer sense of where you stand in the decision process.
