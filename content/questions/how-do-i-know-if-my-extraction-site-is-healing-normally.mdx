Extraction site healing follows a predictable biological sequence. Understanding the normal timeline helps patients recognize expected discomfort versus signs that warrant a call to the surgeon's office.

## The Normal Healing Sequence

**Day 1 — Clot formation.** A blood clot forms in the empty socket within minutes to hours after extraction. This clot is the biological foundation of healing. Some oozing is normal during the first few hours; significant ongoing bleeding should be reported. Avoid rinsing vigorously, spitting forcefully, or using a straw — these actions can displace the clot and expose the bone beneath.

**Days 2–3 — Peak swelling.** Swelling, bruising, and jaw stiffness typically peak within 48–72 hours. This is a normal inflammatory response. Cold packs during the first 24 hours and anti-inflammatory medication (if cleared by your surgeon) can reduce swelling. Pain should be at or below the level controlled by prescribed medication. Pain that is worsening rather than improving after day two may indicate a complication.

**Days 4–7 — Gradual improvement.** Swelling begins to subside. The socket may appear white or yellowish as granulation tissue forms — this is normal healing tissue, not infection. Soft tissue at the margins of the socket starts to close. Some soreness with jaw movement is expected.

**Weeks 2–4 — Soft tissue closure.** The visible opening closes as gum tissue grows across the socket. Bone remodeling continues beneath the surface for three to six months, though it is not visible externally.

## Red Flags That Warrant a Call

**Dry socket (alveolar osteitis).** The most common extraction complication. Signs: severe, throbbing pain beginning three to five days after extraction — often radiating toward the ear or jaw — combined with an empty-looking socket, foul taste, or bad odor. Dry socket is not an infection, but it is painful and requires a follow-up visit. The surgeon applies a medicated dressing directly to the socket to control pain while healing progresses.

**Infection.** Signs: fever above 101°F (38.3°C), swelling that increases after day three, pus or discharge from the socket, difficulty swallowing or opening the mouth. Infections after simple extractions are uncommon but require prompt evaluation.

**Persistent numbness.** Local anesthesia typically resolves within a few hours. Numbness that persists beyond 24–48 hours may indicate nerve involvement and should be documented and reported.

**Incomplete soft tissue closure.** If the socket does not appear to be closing after two to three weeks, or if small sharp fragments (bone spicules) are visible breaking through the gum, contact the surgeon's office for assessment.

## When in Doubt, Call

Extraction sites heal differently based on tooth type, patient health, surgical complexity, and aftercare adherence. If something seems wrong or different from what your surgeon described at discharge, contact the office. Most complications are manageable when identified early.
